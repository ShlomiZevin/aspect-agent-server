## 1. The verdict

It lands as a promise in thirty seconds, but not yet as a scalable company. The weakest link is timing: slide one triggers “AI services firm,” and slide five is the first slide that kills that interpretation. In the gap, the investor mentally prices people, projects and bad margins before your real economic insight arrives.

## 2. Slide by slide

### Slide 1 — Hero

“We are your AI” is bold, ownable and worth protecting; the range from report to entire system makes the ambition clear. The dead weight is “Anything you need,” because nothing says who does the building, so the breadth reads as human labour hiding behind AI. The investor is thinking: “Big promise. Is this a consultancy with an LLM?”

### Slide 2 — The gap

“A scope, a quote, a quarter” and “So you stopped asking” are the strongest parts: recognisable pain, expressed fast. Repeating the output list is wasted space, and “not because it is hard” is both debatable and unnecessary; the sentence about software deciding which questions are allowed is vivid but over-written. The investor is thinking: “Yes, the backlog is real—now show me why this company can remove it.”

### Slide 3 — The starting line

The core insight is excellent: learning the data is the foundation, not a fresh project every time. The body says it three different ways, while “we are connected” sounds like integration work and “quirks nobody wrote down” risks sounding magical rather than rigorous. The investor is thinking: “This is the moat—or an expensive onboarding service.”

### Slide 4 — The offer

“No catalog. No roadmap. Just: what do you need?” is sharp, and the Claude analogy gives investors a category they can repeat. But this is the third time they have seen the report-screen-automation-system list, and generic “Claude” suggests a chatbot when the real analogy is Claude Code: an open-ended builder. The investor is thinking: “Now I understand the idea, but is this more than a wrapper?”

### Slide 5 — Why nobody offered this before

This is the economic unlock and the most investor-relevant slide: vendors were forced to say no because every yes carried labour. “Called it focus” is excellent; keep it. The abstract sentence about a shift “arriving at the business” weakens the punch, and the whole answer arrives too late. The investor is thinking: “Finally—the scalability argument. Now I need to believe the AI really carries the work.”

### Slide 6 — The invitation

“Ask us for something we have never built” is the right ending because it converts breadth into a test. The vendor contrast earns its place, but the live-room promise raises the standard from a strong narrative to a public demonstration, so only make it if you want investors to take you up on it. The investor is thinking: “Good. I want to test the claim—and I will judge the company by what happens next.”

## 5. Writing rules

1. **Write for one breath: most spoken sentences should stay under twelve words.**  
   Before: “That work is done before the first question is ever asked.” → After: “We learn your data up front.”

2. **Use two short beats, one explanation, then a hard close.**  
   Before: “Each one becomes a scope, a quote, a quarter.” → After: “A scope. A quote. A quarter. Everything else waits.”

3. **Put the actor before the action. Never hide behind passive voice.**  
   Before: “It gets built.” → After: “The AI builds it.”

4. **Reach for physical verbs: ask, build, know, learn, count, check, answer.**  
   Before: “The same shift is arriving at the business.” → After: “The AI does the building now.”

5. **Use contrast without explaining the contrast twice.**  
   Before: “Every other vendor sells you what they already built. We build what you ask for.” → Tighter: “They sell what they built. We build what you ask for.”

6. **Repeat only the three words you want remembered: ask, build, data.**  
   Before: Repeat “a report, a screen, an automation, a system” on three slides. → After: Use the list once, then say “what you need.”

7. **End on the consequence, not the mechanism.**  
   Before: “Which is why everything else is fast.” → After: “Every ask starts ahead.”

8. **Use a full stop when the next phrase changes the meaning.**  
   Before: “Anything you need—built on your data.” → After: “You ask. The AI builds it. On your data.”

9. **Delete hedging before a category claim.**  
   Before: “Think of it as Claude, for your business.” → After: “Claude Code for the business.”

10. **Prefer concrete friction to abstract frustration.**  
    Before: “Everything becomes a project.” → After: “A scope. A quote. A quarter.”

11. **Never make one sentence carry both the old world and the new world.**  
    Before: “The same shift that rewrote software is arriving at the business.” → After: “Vendors had to say no. The AI makes yes possible.”

12. **Ban corporate category sludge.**  
    Before: “There is a capability the customer points at what they need.” → After: “You ask. We build.”

## 6. Visual direction

### The typographic idea

Let type make the argument: **the customer asks in regular weight; the answer lands in heavy weight.** Use one blunt sans-serif family—Neue Haas Grotesk, Söhne, or Inter Tight—with no decorative display face. Headlines should sit between 88–128 pt; body copy between 24–30 pt. Eyebrows can use 13–15 pt uppercase with wide tracking.

Keep everything left-aligned. No centered manifesto copy. No italics. No rounded cards. No fake chat interface. The typography should feel like a command followed by a result.

### Text volume and placement

No paragraph should appear as a paragraph. Cap each slide at roughly 30 visible words; slides 1, 4, and 6 should carry fewer than 20. The founder says the explanation. The slide delivers the blow.

Keep the headline in the upper-left quadrant and one short supporting thought near the bottom-left. Leave at least half the canvas empty. Never shrink type to preserve copy.

### Colour

Use three colours only:

- Warm white: `#F2F0E8`
- Near black: `#090909`
- Signal orange: `#FF4A00`

Warm white is the default background. Orange means action: **ask**, **built**, or the decisive turn in the argument. Slide 5 should invert to black to mark the economic break. Avoid blue, purple, gradients, glowing orbs, data-stream graphics, and every other visual shorthand for AI.

### Motion

Use hard cuts between slides. No dissolve, zoom, morph, parallax, or typewriter animation.

Motion should only express sequence. On slide 2, `SCOPE. QUOTE. QUARTER.` may appear in three clicks. On slide 4, the final word `BUILT.` may snap on after the examples. Nothing should travel across the screen. The deck must still work perfectly as a PDF.

### The repeating structural device

Frame every slide with three thin rules: top, left, and bottom. **Never draw the right edge.**

That open side encodes the idea: this is not a closed product with fixed boundaries. The frame remains identical across all six slides, while the content changes inside it. Do not turn it into a box or decorate it with interface elements.

## 7. The kill list

### Cut outright from the current deck

- **“Not because it is hard”** — it invites the investor to argue that some of it is, in fact, hard.
- **“The software you bought decided years ago which questions you are allowed to ask”** — too long and too theatrical. The cleaner accusation is: `Your software only answers the questions it was built for.`
- **“We are connected and we have learned it”** — awkward English. Say: `We already know your data.`
- **“That work is done before the first question is ever asked”** — the headline already says it.
- **“It is not the project — it is the foundation everything else is built on”** — explain this aloud; do not print the explanation beneath the claim.
- **“Which is why everything else is fast”** — generic conclusion. Let the logic land without announcing it.
- **“One place you ask”** — makes the company sound like a chat interface.
- **The four repeated uses of “Built” on slide 4** — one hard `BUILT.` lands better than four sales-demo repetitions.
- **“The same shift that already rewrote how software itself gets made, arriving at the business”** — abstract, long, and difficult to say. Replace it aloud with: `AI changed who can build software. Now it changes what a business can ask for.`
- **One of the repeated example lists** — reports, screens, automations, and systems currently appear on slides 1, 2, and 4. Keep the full list on slides 1 and 4; slide 2 is about delay, not outputs.

## 8. What comes after slide six

**1. “Isn’t this still a services business?”**

There is work up front, but we do not start again with every request. We connect once, learn the data once, and keep that context. From then on, new requests run on the same machinery. A services company scales requests by adding people; we scale them by reusing what the system has already learned. The outputs are custom. The machinery is shared.

**2. “Why won’t Microsoft, Salesforce, or Anthropic do this?”**

Microsoft and Salesforce will build more inside Microsoft and Salesforce. Anthropic will make a better builder. None of them arrives knowing how this business defines a sale across all the systems it already runs. We start from the business, not from one application. Better models make us faster; they do not replace the learned context, the capabilities already built, or the trust we earn around the customer’s data.

**3. “How can you safely say yes to anything?”**

The promise is open-ended; the answers are not unbounded. We only build from the data and permissions the customer actually has. Every number carries the query that produced it, and a separate check runs before it ships. If the evidence is not there, we say so. Saying yes means yes to doing the work—not yes to inventing the answer.
